# Course Design

> **When to read this:** Step 3 (designing the curriculum) and Step 4 (writing modules). These principles decide what goes in the course and how it's taught.

The goal is not a summary of the video. It's a course that leaves the learner **able to do or explain something** they couldn't before, in less time than the video takes to watch.

---

## 1. Start from outcomes

Before planning modules, write 3 outcomes as "After this course you can…" statements. They appear in the hero and drive every module.

- Good: "Explain why starting early beats saving more"
- Good: "Spot the three warning signs of a bad index fund"
- Bad: "Understand investing" (too vague to test)

## 2. Shape: 3–6 modules

| Video length | Modules | Screens per module |
|---|---|---|
| Under 15 min | 3 | 3–4 |
| 15–60 min | 4–6 | 4–6 |
| Over 60 min, or several videos | 5–7 | 5–7 |

Fewer, stronger modules beat many thin ones. Follow the video's logic, not its running order: if the speaker explains the key idea at minute 40, it can still be Module 1.

A reliable arc:
1. **The big idea:** what this is about and why it matters, grounded in the most surprising moment
2. **The building blocks:** core concepts, each with a metaphor
3. **How it works:** the process, steps, or mechanism
4. **In practice:** examples, cases, and stories from the video
5. **Mistakes and edge cases:** what the speaker warns about
6. **Putting it together:** a scenario quiz across all modules, flashcards, and the next step

## 3. Show, don't tell

- **Max 3 sentences** per paragraph, and never two paragraphs in a row. Break them up with a visual component.
- Every screen should be **at least half visual**: cards, steps, compare, concept blocks, quotes.
- A list of 3+ items becomes **cards**. A sequence becomes **steps**. A contrast becomes **compare**.
- **One idea per screen.** If a screen teaches two things, split it.

## 4. Metaphors first, then the video

Introduce each concept with an everyday metaphor, then ground it in what the speaker actually said, with a timestamp. Use a **different metaphor for every concept**; never reuse one.

## 5. Quizzes test doing, not remembering

Every module ends with at least one quiz. Write **scenario questions** that make the learner apply the idea:

- Bad: "What year did the speaker start investing?"
- Good: "Your friend is 35 and thinks it's too late. What's the best reply, based on the video?"

Rules:
- 3 options, one clearly correct, and two wrong options that are tempting (common misconceptions, not jokes).
- The explanation says *why* and links to the moment in the video.
- Mix up which position the correct answer is in.

## 6. Plain English everywhere

- Assume no background knowledge. Wrap every piece of jargon in a glossary `term` the first time it appears.
- Write like a smart friend explaining over coffee, not a textbook.
- Write in the language of the video unless the user asks otherwise.

## 7. Truth rules

- **Only teach what the video says.** You can add a metaphor or reframe for clarity, but never add facts, numbers, or claims the speaker didn't make.
- If the speaker is wrong or outdated on something important, add a `callout warn` saying so briefly, and label it as your note.
- **Quotes are exact.** Clean up filler words ("um", "you know") and fix obvious caption typos, but never change the meaning.
- Every quote and number gets a timestamp link.
